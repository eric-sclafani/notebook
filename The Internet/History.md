
## Beginnings

In the 1960s, the `internet` began as an army-funded research project to share information across computers. Computers in the '60s were large and immobile. The internet started as a way for these large computers to communicate information with each other.

Another push for the internet was the **Cold War**. The Soviet Union's launching of the **Sputnik** satellite spurred the U.S. Defense department to consider ways information can be shared even after a nuclear attack. 

This eventually lead to the creation of the `ARPANET`, (Advanced Research Projects Agency Network), **the network that ultimately evolved into what we now know as the Internet**. The revolutionary system in which data is transferred between computers is called `packet switching`. This process breaks data down into **blocks, or packets**, before sending it to its destination. That way, each packet can take its own route from place to place.

On October 29, 1969, Arpanet delivered its first message: a “node-to-node” communication from one computer to another. The message—“**LOGIN**”—was short and simple, but it crashed the fledgling Arpanet anyway: **The Stanford computer only received the note’s first two letters.**

`January 1st, 1983` is considered the official birthday of the internet. Prior to this date, there was no standard for network communication. A new communications protocol was created and called `Transfer Control Protocol/Internetwork Protocal` (TCP/IP). 

This allowed **different kinds of computers on different networks to "talk" to each other.** ARPANET and the Defense Data Network officially changed to the TCP/IP standard on January 1, 1983, hence the birth of the Internet. All networks could now be connected by a **universal language**.

The internet eventually **evolved into public infrastructure in the 1980s** with the support of many universities and private companies, as other institutions saw the great potential in the internet's usefulness.

## Early browsers

British computer scientist **Tim Berners-Lee** created the first web server and graphical web browser in 1990 while working at CERN, the European Organization for Nuclear Research, in Switzerland. He called his new window into the internet `WorldWideWeb`.

In 1993, because of the rising popularity of the internet, the browser called `Mosaic` was created at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA) at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign by computer scientist Marc Andreessen. 

The next year, Andreessen created another browser called `Netscape Navigator`. This browser became wildly popular, and is the ancestor of `Mozilla FireFox`. From here, the competition to create browsers began!

## The Browser Wars

By 1995, **Microsoft** licensed the old Mosaic code and created `Internet Explorer` (IE). With this release, the competition was on. Netscape and IE were competing to see who can outdo the other with faster, better products.

Netscape created and released `Javascript`, written by **Brendan Eich**. This provided browsers with powerful computing capabilities. 

Microsoft retaliated by creating `Cascading Style Sheets` (CSS) which became the standard for web page design.

Microsoft started shipping IE as the default web browser with their Windows operation system. This secured them a market majority, which they then faced antitrust litigation over. Netscape, not wanting to follow in Microsoft's monopoly footsteps, **open sourced** their codebase and created a non-profit called `Mozilla`. They later released `Firefox` in 2002.

By 2010, Firefox, Safari, Google Chrome, Opera, and Microsoft Edge (IE successor) all existed and continue to thrive.
